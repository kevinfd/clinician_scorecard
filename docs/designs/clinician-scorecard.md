## Problem Statement

A neurosurgery department inside MGB has written a v0.2 spec for ~25 per-surgeon metrics across six buckets (volume, efficiency, access, quality, patient experience, citizenship), sourced from eight institutional systems (OR log, Vizient, department QI database, MGB patient survey, billing, clinic scheduling, referrals, a new QR-code M&M attendance system). Today a surgeon sees almost none of these numbers about themselves: patient-experience scores arrive twice a year at faculty meeting, M&M attendance and wRVUs matter to the comp plan, and the rest is assembled by hand, if at all, when a chief or chair needs it.

**What is actually being built (restated after the framing check):** not a scorecard. There is no composite score, no rank, and no target except M&M attendance. It is a read-only, per-surgeon metrics viewer with (a) a record-level attribution ledger behind every number, (b) a dispute path to the chief or chair, (c) minimum-n and minimum-peer-group suppression that explains itself, (d) anonymous peer spread, and (e) trend on every metric. The charts are the surface. The attribution ledger and the dispute path are the product, because those are what make a surgeon accept a number in a comp or OPPE conversation.

**Framing check (three axes):**
- Language precision. "Scorecard" implies a score; the brief has none. "Peer group" is undefined in size; the brief's own rule (hide if fewer than five surgeons) means many site-level subspecialty comparisons will never render in a department of neurosurgery scale. "Anonymous" is not defined against re-identification: with five peers and visible volumes, everyone knows who is who.
- Hidden assumptions. (1) Each of the eight feeds is obtainable at surgeon-level attribution by this department; (2) Vizient's expected values can be re-attributed to the index surgeon rather than the discharging attending; (3) the QR attendance system exists and will be live for the fiscal year; (4) a surgeon will open a web page they were not required to open.
- Real vs hypothetical. The brief is a spec written in the second person ("you can move it by"). No surgeon is quoted. The only evidenced pain is contractual (comp plan counts M&M attendance) and cadence ("available any time instead of twice a year").

**Failure patterns named:** platform-first (eight feeds and 25 metrics before one surgeon sees one real number); hypothetical user (the "you" in the brief is a category, not a person); assuming interest equals demand for the quality bucket; and the documented report-card failure mode (risk aversion, case-selection gaming, mistrust of attribution) that the brief's ground rules are designed against but have not yet been tested against a real surgeon.

## Constraints

## Prior Art and Conventional Wisdom

Search ran with generalized category terms only.

- Layer 1 (tried and true): OPPE/FPPE is a Joint Commission requirement, and attribution accuracy is what gives peer review credibility (Credentialing Resource Center). Vizient CDB already produces physician-level mortality, LOS, readmission, and complication O/E with drill-down (Vizient CDB). Surgeon-specific report cards reliably cause risk aversion and case-selection gaming when results are published or ranked (Health Affairs 2015; NY cardiac surgery literature). At low per-surgeon volumes, registry data rarely detects individual outliers (statewide collaborative study).
- Layer 2 (current discourse): balanced scorecards tied to comp; the observation that "if RVUs update daily while quality lags by months, volume will always win"; email attachments reduce engagement versus on-demand portals; and evidence that anonymous FPPE processes are eroding OR trust (57% of surveyed surgeons avoided high-risk cases in relation to anonymous reporting).
- Layer 3 (first principles, from this brief): the brief already contains the corrections the literature asks for (O/E where a risk model exists, "unadjusted" labels, min-n, no rank, dispute path, drill to records). The conventional wisdom is sound on those. Where it is wrong here: the anonymous peer bar chart. EUREKA candidate: everyone builds anonymous peer comparison because they assume anonymity protects the surgeon. In a five-surgeon subspecialty group where everyone knows each other's volume, anonymity is fake; it removes the useful conversation (who does this well, ask them) without preventing identification. Implication: either raise the anonymity threshold above five, or for small groups drop the peer spread and show self-trend plus the external Vizient benchmark. Recorded as Premise 5 for the human to dispute.

## Premises

Provisionally accepted for this run. Each is falsifiable. Dispute any of them and the design changes.

1. The department can obtain a case-level OR log extract (primary surgeon, scheduled and actual times, booked duration, block, delay reason, cancellation reason code) within 30 days, without an enterprise IT project.
2. Every OR-log case has exactly one primary surgeon that maps to the department roster; ambiguous or co-surgeon cases are rare enough (under 2%) to handle by dispute rather than by design.
3. Vizient expected values can be re-attributed to the index surgeon as the brief requires, rather than Vizient's default attending attribution; if not, quality O/E metrics need a different expected-value source.
4. At least one subspecialty at one site has five or more surgeons, so at least one site-level peer comparison can ever render. If false, site-level peer groups should be dropped from the spec.
5. Anonymous peer spread in groups of five to eight does not prevent re-identification, so it does not deliver the protection it promises. (Eureka candidate; dispute this if the department's experience says otherwise.)
6. Surgeons will accept a number they can trace to a record list and dispute; without that path, the same number is rejected. The trust mechanics, not the charts, drive adoption.
7. The department is permitted to host a surgeon-identified, PHI-derived internal web page behind MGB single sign-on, or such approval is obtainable inside one quarter.
8. The comp plan will consume M&M attendance and wRVU from this system, which is what keeps it alive after the champion leaves. If the comp plan keeps its own separate count, this system is optional and dies at the first reorg.
9. Twice-a-year delivery of patient-experience scores is a felt pain; more frequent access will be used more than twice a year. Currently no evidence either way.
10. Doing nothing has a real cost: chiefs assemble numbers by hand for comp and OPPE, and disputes are argued without record-level backing. Cost in hours is unmeasured.

## Approaches Considered

### Approach A: One feed, one subspecialty, static pages (minimal viable)
- Summary: monthly batch job reads an OR-log extract, applies metric definitions written as code (one function per metric with its min-n, peer group, and definition text), and emits one page per surgeon for the five OR-log metrics with trend, anonymous spread, drill-to-case-list, "why not shown" reasons, and a dispute link (mailto or form to the chief). Pages sit behind existing MGB SSO on an internal host the department already has. A monthly email carries each surgeon's numbers and a deep link.
- Effort: S. Human 3-4 weeks; AI-assisted about 1 week once the extract is in hand.
- Risk: Low technically; Medium on feed access.
- Pros: real surgeons see real numbers inside a month; exercises every ground rule on low-stakes metrics; the metric-definitions-as-code and the attribution ledger schema carry forward to B; nothing to host beyond static files.
- Cons: no dispute state machine, no roles beyond surgeon, no feedback inbox, no cross-feed metrics; anonymity of five-peer spread is weak (Premise 5); trend needs history the extract may not carry.
- Reuses: the brief's definitions verbatim as docstrings; the existing SSO and internal hosting; the existing dispute route (chief, then chair).
- Completeness: 5/10 (happy path for one bucket, suppression and drill-down done properly).

### Approach B: Attribution ledger, metric engine, role-based web app (ideal architecture)
- Summary: nightly ETL from all eight feeds into a record-level attribution ledger (every case, visit, admission, survey, attendance record with clinician, source, version, and dispute state); a metric engine that computes every metric in the brief from the ledger with versioned definitions; a web app with surgeon, direct-leader, chief, chair, division, and site views; dispute workflow with audit trail; feedback inbox with private notes; M&M pace tracker; wRVU self-tracker with billing-lag as-of date.
- Effort: XL. Human 6-12 months with an analyst and an engineer; AI-assisted 2-3 months of build, but the calendar is set by feed access and governance, not code.
- Risk: High. Eight feed owners, PHI governance, Vizient re-attribution (Premise 3), and the QR system not yet existing.
- Pros: the thing the brief describes; survives the champion if the comp plan and OPPE consume it (Premise 8); ledger-first design makes disputes and drill-down native rather than bolted on.
- Cons: no surgeon sees anything for months; greenlight has to be won on a promise; every unresolved definition in the brief blocks a bucket; highest chance of dying in a reorg before launch.
- Reuses: Vizient CDB O/E and risk weights; the QI database as sole complication source; the patient survey feed in its existing faculty-meeting shape.
- Completeness: 9/10.

### Approach C: Build inside the enterprise BI tool the department already has (lateral)
- Summary: implement the metric views as dashboards in whatever BI or Epic-adjacent reporting tool MGB already licenses and governs, rather than a custom app. Distribution, SSO, and PHI governance are already solved there.
- Effort: M. Human 6-10 weeks; AI-assisted 2-4 weeks, gated on tool access.
- Risk: Medium.
- Pros: fastest path to sanctioned hosting; analysts already know the tool; enterprise data feeds may already be connected.
- Cons: dispute workflow, private notes, feedback inbox, and "every number links to its definition and record list" are awkward or impossible in most BI tools; tool lock-in; department loses control of definitions to the BI team.
- Reuses: enterprise SSO, hosting, and possibly existing Vizient and billing connections.
- Completeness: 6/10 (charts strong, trust mechanics weak).

Ruled out: a composite score or rank (the brief forbids it and the literature says it causes case-selection gaming). Starting with the quality bucket (highest stakes, weakest attribution, smallest n; wrong place to meet surgeons for the first time).

## Recommended Approach

Approach A, designed so its metric-definitions-as-code and ledger schema are the first module of B.

- Why A: the intrapreneurship goal is a greenlight. A sponsor greenlights what they can see working with real surgeons' data, and the OR log is the one feed a surgical department can plausibly get this month. Showing FCOT with a disputable delay reason, min-n suppression that explains itself, and a case list behind every count proves the brief's ground rules before asking for feeds two through eight.
- Why not B first: months of no visible output is how internal tools die before launch.
- Why not C: the trust mechanics (drill-to-records, dispute, private notes) are the product, and BI tools do them badly.
- Build order inside A: attribution ledger schema and metric functions first (they are reused), pages second, email third.
- Anonymity rule for A: follow the brief (hide under five peers) but log how often the spread renders; that number decides Premise 5.
- No wireframe was produced in this run (headless, no-code rule). A 1-3 screen rough sketch should precede /plan-design-review.

## Success Criteria

- Greenlight: within 30 days of the A demo, the sponsor authorizes access to a second feed (QI database or Vizient) in writing.
- Adoption: all surgeons in the pilot subspecialty (five or more) open their page at least once in the first month; at least three open a drill-to-case list.
- Trust: at least one dispute is filed and resolved within 14 days, with the resolution reflected in the record list; every attribution error a surgeon finds is reproducible from the case list (zero "the number is just wrong" outcomes).
- Suppression: 100% of below-threshold cells show a reason, none show a blank.
- Freshness: monthly refresh within 10 business days of month close, for three consecutive months.
- Survival: metric definitions versioned in the repo and a written feed-access agreement naming a role, not a person, before A is called done.

## What I noticed about how you think

Quoted from the brief, since no human was present.
